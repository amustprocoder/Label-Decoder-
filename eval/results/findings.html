Indian Label Decoder

Indian nutrition labels are legally correct and practically useless. Paste the pack and get the real picture: sugar per serve in teaspoons, whether each front-of-pack claim survives per-serving maths, which ingredients are the same thing under different names, and the FSSAI rule the pack is following while misleading you.

How it works. A vision model only transcribes the pack into a fixed JSON form. Every judgement comes from a rules layer written as code: 60+ sugar aliases in English, Hindi transliteration and INS numbers; the FSSAI Advertising and Claims Regulations 2018 Schedule I thresholds for low, free, source-of and high claims; per-100 g to per-serve conversion; 18 mislead patterns, each citing the regulation it leans on. The same label always gives the same verdict, and transcription errors and rule errors are measured separately.

    A chocolate confectionery with an 18 g declared serve. Sugar is the first ingredient; the list then adds lactose-rich whey permeate and maltodextrin, which are sugar and sugar-like under other names. A tiny serve keeps every per-serve number small.

Legally fine (ingredients only need descending order of weight); practically it keeps 'sugar' off the top of the list. (FSS (Labelling & Display) Regs 2020, Reg 5(2): ingredients in descending order of weight)

    • low 34% of energy from saturated fat. 18 g saturated fat per 100 ml. (WHO: keep saturated fat under 10% of energy)

      Where it breaks

      Clean packs read at 96%. Crumpled film, glare through plastic, curved tubs and bottles, US-style per-serve-only panels and ingredient strips in tiny type pull the model to 66%. Dietary fibre and sodium are the weakest fields: fibre is often printed as a bound ("<1 g") or in the last row that gets cropped, and sodium arrives as salt, as "0.1 g", or as a per-serve value.

      The app

      Three ways in: pack photos, pasted label text, or a barcode (Open Food Facts lookup). Barcode and the 100 built-in examples work with no model key; photo and text use Claude or the free Gemini tier.

      Method and limits

      Thresholds follow the FSS (Advertising and Claims) Regulations 2018 and the FSS (Labelling and Display) Regulations 2020 as published; they are amended from time to time and should be re-verified before quoting publicly. The reference data is Open Food Facts crowd entries that passed a sanity filter; some of the 13% extraction "errors" are reference mistakes, which a hand-checked label sheet in the repository is designed to correct. The vision transcriptions for this evaluation were produced by Claude Sonnet reading the 300 pack photos; the application calls the same model through its API. Pack photos are from Open Food Facts (CC BY-SA).
